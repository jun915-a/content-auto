When autovacuum or checkpoint processes consume excessive CPU, or when temporary files fill up the disk, the database stalls. Parameters like `shared_buffers`, `work_mem`, or `max_connections` interact in unpredictable ways. Similarly, OS-level settings like swappiness or `vm.max_map_count` can sabotage stability if ignored. These issues often lurk undetected until a crisis forces them into the spotlight.\n\n> 💡 Insight: The most dangerous misconfigurations are those that *seem* correct but introduce subtle fragility. A setting that works in a lab may fail spectacularly under real-world load patterns.\n\n**Blocking queries**\nLong-running transactions or unindexed queries can lock tables indefinitely, freezing entire applications. Even a single ill-optimized query—like a `SELECT *` on a large table without proper indexing—can cascade into system-wide gridlock. Tools like `pg_blocking_pids` or `pg_locks` are essential for diagnosing these issues, but many teams overlook their importance until a critical outage reveals the problem.\n\n**Replication lag**\nSynchronous replication isn’t a silver bullet. Lag between primary and standby servers introduces data staleness, which can break applications relying on up-to-date reads. Worse, if the primary fails during a lag spike, failover may expose stale data or trigger cascading failures. Detecting replication lag early—and understanding its root causes (e.g., network latency, disk I/O bottlenecks)—is critical to maintaining system reliability.",
