# I Want a Wife: A Scathing Satire on Gender Roles

"I Want a Wife" (1971) is a razor-sharp essay that exposes the unpaid labor and societal expectations women endure. Through a fictional job application, Judy Brady dismantles the myth of domestic bliss, demanding recognition for the invisible work of marriage. A timeless critique of gender inequality.

## 🔑 The Core of This Topic
A 1971 essay by Judy Brady, "I Want a Wife" is a fictional job advertisement parodying the unspoken contract of marriage. Brady frames her request for a wife as a job application, listing the exhaustive, often unpaid responsibilities expected of women—cooking, cleaning, child-rearing, emotional labor—while highlighting how these tasks are rarely valued or compensated. The essay is a biting critique of gender roles, revealing how society assumes women will fulfill these roles without acknowledgment or fairness.

## ⚡ 5-Second Key Points
- **Point 1**: **Fictional job ad** exposes the hidden labor of marriage as an unpaid job.
- **Point 2**: **Gender inequality** is laid bare through Brady’s satirical list of domestic duties.
- **Point 3**: **No recognition**—women’s contributions are normalized as selfless, not professional.

## 📈 Detailed Breakdown
**Element 1**
Brady’s essay begins with a **job advertisement** for a wife, framing marriage as an employment contract. She lists duties like "getting married, bearing children, keeping house, obeying my husband, and loving him"—tasks that, in reality, are expected of women without compensation. This structure forces readers to confront how these responsibilities are **normalized as natural**, rather than labor that demands time, skill, and energy. The essay’s humor and irony underscore the absurdity of treating these roles as voluntary rather than systemic.

**Element 2**
The essay’s power lies in its **specificity**. Brady doesn’t just generalize about women’s burdens; she **itemizes** them—"I want a wife who will not object when I come home ten minutes late from work," or "I want a wife who will keep me in touch with my family." Each line reveals how society **expects women to absorb emotional and physical labor** while men remain exempt from similar demands. The tone shifts from playful to **undeniably urgent**, exposing the **double standards** that underpin traditional marriage.

> 💡 Insight: **The essay’s brilliance is in its simplicity**—by treating marriage as a job, Brady forces readers to quantify the **invisible work** women perform daily, proving that love and duty are not enough to sustain such an unequal arrangement.

## 🎯 Real-World Impact
- **Challenged gender norms**: The essay became a **foundational text** in feminist discourse, influencing movements advocating for **equal domestic responsibility** and **paid labor recognition** for caregiving.
- **Inspired activism**: It fueled discussions about **unpaid labor statistics**, showing how women’s contributions to households are **undervalued** in economic terms.
- **Still relevant today**: Despite progress, the essay’s critique resonates in debates over **work-life balance**, **parental leave**, and **gender pay gaps**, proving its enduring relevance.

## ✨ Conclusion
Judy Brady’s "I Want a Wife" remains a **timeless weapon** in the fight for gender equity. By exposing the **unpaid, unappreciated labor** of marriage, she challenges readers to question the **fairness of societal expectations**. Nearly five decades later, her words continue to spark conversations about **fairness, recognition, and the cost of love**—reminding us that **no one should have to apply for the right to be treated with basic equity**.
