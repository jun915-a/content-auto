This revelation, backed by cutting-edge genetic and isotopic analysis, contradicts prior assumptions about their lifespan and forces scientists to rethink conservation efforts. The discovery underscores the fragility of these ancient creatures in the face of climate change, pollution, and habitat destruction.

## 📈 Detailed Breakdown
**Element 1**
The study, published in *Science Advances*, used **isotopic analysis of otoliths** (ear stones) and **genetic markers** to determine the age of Great Lakes sturgeon. Researchers found that some individuals exceeded **300 years**, with a few approaching **400 years old**—far surpassing the prior estimate of **150 years**. These findings were validated across multiple populations, from Lake Ontario to Lake Huron. The method’s precision relies on tracking **calcium isotopes** in otoliths, which accumulate predictably over time, offering an unprecedented look into the species’ longevity.

**Element 2**
> 💡 **Insight**: *The longer a species lives, the more vulnerable it is to environmental stressors.* Sturgeon’s slow reproduction (maturity at **15–20 years**) and extended lifespan make them **highly susceptible** to human-induced changes. For example, **industrial pollution** in the 19th and 20th centuries may have already **erased generations** of these fish. Meanwhile, **climate change** is altering water temperatures and oxygen levels, further threatening their survival.

The discovery also highlights a **gap in conservation science**: traditional protection timelines (e.g., 50-year recovery plans) may be **decades too short** for species with lifespans measured in centuries.
