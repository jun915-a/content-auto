## 📈 Detailed Breakdown
**Franklin’s Revolutionary Methodology**
Rosalind Franklin’s expertise in **X-ray crystallography** was unparalleled. At King’s College London, she refined techniques to capture high-resolution images of DNA fibers, isolating the molecule’s structure with unprecedented clarity. Unlike her male colleagues, she insisted on **high-quality data** over speculative models, ensuring Photo 51’s accuracy. Her work proved DNA was a **double helix**—a helical structure with two strands—long before Watson and Crick’s theoretical leap.

> 💡 Insight: **Franklin’s rigor saved Watson and Crick from error**. Had they relied solely on less precise models (like Linus Pauling’s triple-helix guess), their discovery might have been flawed or delayed.

**The Watson-Crick Exploitation**
Watson and Crick’s 1953 paper credited Franklin only vaguely, calling her work ‘unpublished.’ In his 1962 memoir, Watson wrote that Franklin ‘didn’t understand’ her own data—a claim later contradicted by **her lab notebooks**, which showed she had already deduced the double-helix structure. Franklin’s supervisor, **John Randall**, withheld her findings, prioritizing institutional prestige over her contributions. Even today, **textbooks often omit her name**, reinforcing the ‘Watson and Crick’ myth.

**Legacy and Modern Reckoning**
Franklin’s story reflects systemic barriers faced by women in science. Her **posthumous recognition**—including the 2013 unveiling of a DNA model at the **Science Museum London** (with her name prominently displayed)—marks a slow but necessary correction. Today, her work is celebrated as a **paradigm of scientific integrity**, proving that **data, not ego, drives discovery**.

## 🎯 Real-World Impact
- **Ethical Lessons**: Franklin’s case highlights the dangers of **credit theft in science**, prompting modern institutions to prioritize transparency and equity in acknowledgment.
