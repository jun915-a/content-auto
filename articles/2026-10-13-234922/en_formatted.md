## 🔑 The Core of This Topic
A student-led MIT project designed to address tremors in Parkinson’s and essential tremor patients has transformed into **Deep Brain Stimulation (DBS) technology**, now FDA-cleared as a **non-invasive alternative** to traditional surgical treatments. This breakthrough bridges engineering ingenuity and medical necessity, proving how academic curiosity can revolutionize healthcare.

- **Point 3**: **FDA approval** marks a milestone for tremor patients seeking safer, accessible treatment.

## 📈 Detailed Breakdown
**Element 1**
The project began as a **MIT course assignment** in biomedical engineering, where students aimed to create a low-cost, portable device to control tremors. Inspired by deep brain stimulation (DBS), they designed a **non-invasive neural modulator** using advanced sensors and algorithms. Unlike traditional DBS, which requires implanted electrodes, their prototype relied on **external electrodes** placed on the scalp, delivering targeted electrical pulses to regulate abnormal brain activity.

**Element 2**
Over five years of iteration, the team refined their invention with input from neurologists and engineers. Key advancements included **adaptive feedback loops** to adjust stimulation in real-time and **user-friendly interfaces** for at-home use. Collaborations with **Massachusetts General Hospital** validated the device’s efficacy in clinical trials, demonstrating **80% reduction in tremor severity** in test subjects. This success caught the attention of **FDA reviewers**, who recognized its potential as a **safer, non-surgical option** for patients who couldn’t undergo traditional DBS.

> 💡 Insight: **This project showcases the power of interdisciplinary collaboration**, merging engineering innovation with medical expertise to address unmet needs in neurology.

## 🎯 Real-World Impact
- **Impact 1**: **Millions of tremor patients**—particularly those with **Parkinson’s disease or essential tremor**—now have a **less invasive alternative** to invasive DBS surgery, reducing risks like infection or hardware failure.
- **Impact 2**: The technology could **lower healthcare costs** by eliminating the need for hospital-based procedures, making advanced treatments more **accessible globally**.
- **Impact 3**: It **inspires future student innovators** to pursue medical breakthroughs, proving that **classroom ideas can save lives**.
