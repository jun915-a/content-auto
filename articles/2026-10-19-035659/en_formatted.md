## 🔑 The Core of This Topic
Dogs, like humans, seem to instinctively link the syllable **‘bouba’** with round, smooth shapes and **‘kiki’** with sharp, angular ones. This discovery suggests a shared cognitive framework for shape-sound associations across species, challenging assumptions about human uniqueness in perception. The study bridges linguistics and animal cognition, hinting at universal patterns in how we interpret the world.

## 📈 Detailed Breakdown
**Element 1**
The **‘bouba-kiki’ effect** is a well-documented phenomenon in human psychology, where people universally pair the syllable **‘bouba’** with blobby, rounded shapes and **‘kiki’** with jagged, angular ones. Researchers at the University of Florence wanted to test whether dogs exhibit a similar bias. Using **3D-printed objects**—some round and smooth, others spiky and angular—they observed how dogs interacted with them while hearing the two syllables. The results were striking: dogs consistently approached the **‘bouba’-labeled objects** first, suggesting an innate preference for roundness, just as humans do.

**Element 2**
The experiment’s design was simple yet ingenious. Dogs were presented with **two objects at a time**: one labeled with **‘bouba’** and the other with **‘kiki’**. Trainers recorded which object the dog sniffed or pawed first. Across multiple trials, the pattern held—**round objects were consistently associated with ‘bouba’**, while spiky ones aligned with **‘kiki’**. This consistency implies that the link between sound and shape may be **hardwired**, not learned through culture or training.

> 💡 Insight: *If dogs share this cognitive quirk with humans, it suggests that shape-sound associations might be a fundamental aspect of perception, not limited to language-dependent species like ours.*
