Whether it’s lottery numbers, AI decisions, or even your own choices, this experiment forces us to question what we assume is random—and what might be rigged.

## ⚡ 5-Second Key Points
- **Point 1**: 'Random' selections often hide predictable biases, even when algorithms claim neutrality.
- **Point 2**: Human perception of randomness is flawed—we overlook patterns until they’re exposed.
- **Point 3**: Tools like this can audit fairness in algorithms, lotteries, or even psychological experiments.

## 📈 Detailed Breakdown
**Element 1**
The Rigged Draw tool simulates randomness by generating sequences of selections (e.g., lottery numbers, dice rolls) and then overlays hidden patterns. Users can tweak parameters like bias strength or sequence length to see how 'randomness' breaks down. The visual feedback—like clustering or gaps—reveals where the system deviates from true randomness. This isn’t just a gimmick; it’s a mirror for how algorithms (or humans) might manipulate perceived fairness.

**Element 2**
> 💡 Insight: **Randomness is a spectrum.** Even small biases accumulate over time, skewing outcomes in ways that feel arbitrary but aren’t. For example, a 'fair' lottery might still favor certain numbers due to subconscious human input or algorithmic quirks. The tool exposes these flaws, prompting us to demand transparency in systems that claim randomness.

## 📈 Detailed Breakdown (Continued)
**Element 3**
The psychological angle is critical: humans *expect* randomness to be uniform, so we ignore deviations until they’re forced into our view. Rigged Draw exploits this by making patterns visually obvious, challenging our intuition. This has parallels in AI ethics—if an algorithm’s 'randomness' is actually biased, who’s accountable? The tool serves as a litmus test for trust in automated systems.
